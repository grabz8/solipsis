\section{Related Works}

The Web3D consortium have originally the ambition to create a freely navigable online world \cite{pesce}. Unfortunately, due to technical constraints, such as bandwidth limitations, the VRML standard was only use to encode simple 3D contents in order to visualize them on web sites. Only, related research and bandwidth increase make it possible nowadays to draft the architecture of a Web3D, metavers or cyberspace. We demonstrate next why decentralized architecture are obviously necessary to reach a sufficient level of scalability.
\subsection{Compression and adaptive 3D Streaming}
Many research works have been focused on compression of 3D models, and on adaptive streaming methods that allow a progressive and fast transmission over networks of the required 3D contents visualized from the current viewpoint. \\
First, existing 3D compression algorithms use both techniques adapted from the 1D and 2D cases (like wavelets, entropy coding, and predictive coding), and completely different approaches that take advantage of the properties of 3D surfaces (like Edgebreaker, Subdivision Surfaces, and triangle strips)\cite{gioia}\cite{isenburg}. Otherwise, parametric solutions, that provide intuitive modeling tools, are widely used to create avatars, complex creatures, or vehicles in games. More complex 3D contents can be create thanks to procedural modeling solutions that have the drawback to require a reconstruction process executed on the fly on the client side. Parametric and procedural modeling provide very good compression rate compared to usual mesh compression algorithms, but no generic solutions exist to model a great variety of objects.\\ 
The second solution consists in filtering the 3D contents required to visualize the scene from a given viewpoint. Indeed, a complete download of a huge 3D scene is not necessary to render with optimal details the virtual environment from a given viewpoint. First, the area of a 3D model projected on the screen depends on its size, but especially on its distance from the viewpoint. In this way, many solution have been proposed to adapt the resolution of a 3D models to the current viewpoint \cite{Funkhouser}. Continuous levels of detail allow to transmit refinements progressively as the viewpoint come closer to 3D objects \cite{Hoppe}\cite{Luebke}. Secondly, into huge and complex 3D scenes, most of 3D objects are occulted by other ones during the navigation. Thanks to a offline computation of 3D objects visible from regions resulting from a partitioning of the navigation area, server can signifanctly reduce the amount of data that have to be sent to the client, without affecting the visual quality \cite{teller}\cite{wonka}. Unfortunately, visibility filtering methods have to be prohibit during flying over navigation, since occultations are clearly limited. In fact, these filtering methods provide multi-resolution functionalities allowing an adaptive 3D streaming of the virtual environments.\\
\subsection{Centralized architectures}
Several architectures take advantage of filtering methods presented previously \cite{npsnet}\cite{massive}\cite{ring}\cite{dive}. Commercial platforms such as Google Earth and Second Life have recentky known a tremendous craze from the public. The first one allows navigation into and over a well-detailed model of the earth, with terrain and buildings. Google earth takes advantage of an adaptive streaming of terrain textures \cite{tanner}, associated with a multi-resolution wavelet based representation for terrain, and static levels of detail for buildings. The second one provides an advanced level of a social network service combined with general aspects of a metavers. The Second Life client integrates modeling tools allowing the creation of the virtual environments by the users. More than fifty online virtual worlds appeared these last years, and are usually based on centralized architectures.\\
Generally, huge environments are partitioned according to a grid in regions that are managed by dedicated servers, called region servers. Unfortunately, in order to synchronize the game states of the world for all connected clients, most of process such as collision detection, physics computation, animation, are executed on the server side. Moreover, the region server is the only source that can provide to clients the 3D models of the scene required for visualization. Thus, the number of clients, which can navigate into a region managed by only one server, is clearly limited.

\subsection{Peer to Peer Architectures for Virtual Worlds}
A centralized architecture cannot lead to a truly self-scalable solution, even with the use of multiple servers. Indeed, client-server architectures lead to prohibitive deployment and maintenance costs when it comes to very large scale applications with thousands of connected clients. On the other hand, thanks to their \emph{Self-Adaptation} features, P2P network overlays have clearly proved that they are equivalent to powerful servers. Based on the fact that if peers have nearly the same viewpoint, they are likely to need the same data, geometric proximity is obviously the main criterion for setting up peers connectivity within virtual environments. Finding and maintaining the appropriate peers connectivity is a very difficult problem in a dynamic environment where peers viewpoint are allowed to move freely and peers can disconnect or appear at any time (this dynamic is called churn in peer-to-peer terminology). Solipsis \cite{keller} and VON \cite{hu} are the first network organization methods that provide a solution for connectivity in 2D environments. In these P2P architectures, peers are connected to each other according to their current 2D location. Dedicated algorithms are used to achieve the global stability of the P2P network while fulfilling a global connectivity constraint (i.e. there must exist at least one path between each pair of peers). Maintain in real-time peers connectivity in a N-dimensionnal space is much more complex, but thanks to this solution, proximity criterion could be extended to semantic or social proximity. Douglas et al. have proposed a region based approach using a distributed spatial data index in a multidimensional space to find nearby objects with which direct connections can be formed \cite{douglas}. Finally, Cavagna et al. show that server-less P2P networks can efficiently deal with very large environments by first using well-suited descriptors to specify areas on interest for continuous levels of detail (for on-demand streaming), and by secondly exchanging between peers their own serving capabilities (for a self-regulation of peer upload bandwidth) \cite{cavagna}.
