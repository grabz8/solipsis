\section{Related Works}

The Web3D consortium have originally the project to create a freely navigable online world \cite{pesce}. Unfortunately, due to technical constraints, such as bandwidth limitations, the VRML standard was only use to encode simple 3D contents in order to visualize them on web sites. Only, related works and bandwidth increase make it possible to draft the architecture of a Web3D, metavers or cyberspace that have to be decentralized to reach a sufficient scalability level.
\subsection{Compression and adaptive 3D Streaming}
Many research works have been focused on compression of 3D models, and on adaptive streaming methods that allow a progressive transmission of the required 3D contents visualized from the current viewpoint. \\
First, existing 3D compression algorithms use both techniques adapted from the 1D and 2D cases (like wavelets, entropy coding, and predictive coding), and completely different approaches that take advantage of the properties of 3D surfaces (like Edgebreaker, Subdivision Surfaces, and triangle strips)\cite{}\cite{}. Otherwise, parametric solutions, that provide intuitive modeling tools, are widely used to create avatars, complex creatures, or vehicles in games. More complex 3D contents can be create thanks to procedural modeling solutions that have the drawback to require a reconstruction process executed on the fly on the client side. Parametric and procedural modeling provide very good compression rate compared to usual mesh compression algorithms, but no generic solutions exist to model a great variety of objects.\\ 
The second solution consists in filtering the 3D contents required to visualize the scene from a given viewpoint. Indeed, a complete download of a huge 3D scene is not necessary to render with optimal details the virtual environment from a given viewpoint. First, the area of a 3D model projected on the screen depends on its size, but especially on its distance from the viewpoint. In this way, many solution have been proposed to adapt the resolution of a 3D models to the current viewpoint \cite{Funkhouser}. Continuous levels of detail allow to transmit refinement progressively as the viewpoint approaches 3D objects \cite{Hoppe}\cite{Luebke}. Secondly, into huge and complex 3D scenes, most of 3D objects are occulted by other ones for a given viewpoint. Thanks to a offline computation of 3D objects visible from regions resulting from a partitioning of the navigation area, server can signifanctly reduce the amount of data that have to be sent to the client, without affecting the visual quality \cite{}\cite{}. Unfortunately, visibility filtering methods have to be prohibit during flying over navigation, since occultations are clearly fewer. Finally, these filtering methods provide multi-resolution functionalities allowing an adaptive 3D streaming of the virtual environments.\\
\subsection{Centralized architectures}
Several architectures take advantage of filtering methods presented previously \cite{npsnet}\cite{massive}\cite{ring}\cite{dive}. More recent commercial platforms such as Google Earth and Second Life have known a tremendous success, and have brought to light a real public interest for online virtual environments. The first one allows navigation into and over a well-detailed model of the earth, with terrain and buildings. Google earth takes advantage of an adaptive streaming of terrain textures \cite{tanner}, associated with a multi-resolution wavelet based representation for terrain, and static levels of detail for buildings. The second one provides an advanced level of a social network service combined with general aspects of a metavers. The Second Life client integrates modeling tools allowing the creation of the virtual environments by the users. More than fifty online virtual worlds appeared these last years, and all are based on a centralized architecture.\\
Generally, huge environments are partitioned according to a grid in regions that are managed by dedicated servers, called region servers. Unfortunately, in order to synchronize the game states of the world for all connected clients, most of process such as collision detection, physics computation, animation, are executed on the server side. Moreover, the clients have only one source that can provide them the 3D models of the scene required for visualization. Thus, the number of clients, which can navigate into a region managed by one server, is clearly limited. Actually, the only solution based on centralized architecture to scale up virtual worlds consists in increasing the number of costly servers, which does not solved the problem of the scalability.  

\subsection{Peer to Peer Architectures for Virtual Worlds}
In order to distribute the load of a metavers, Web3D or cyberspace, in terms of memory, bandwith and computation ressources, peer to peer architectures provide solutions that answer our problem of scalability. Based on the fact that two close viewpoints in a virtual world require approximatively the same data to visualize the scene, some research works proposed peer to peer solutions where 3D contents are associated to peers that are connected together according to their proximity in the virtual 3D space. But as 3D contents such as avatars can move in the virtual environment, peers connexity have to be frequently updated, and this computation have to be local to distribute it on every peers. Keller et al. were the first to propose a peer-to-peer scheme that maintains local properties ensuring the consistency of the virtual world and the connexity of the network \cite{keller}\cite{keller2003}. Shun-Yun Hu et al. have presented a method based on a 2D Voronoi-based Overlay Network called VON that maintains the peer-to-peer topology in a fully-distributed, low-latency, and message efficient manner \cite{hu}. These previous solutions are restricted to 2D overlay to be able to mainatisn on the fly peer-to-peer connexity. Douglas et al. have proposed a region based approach using a distributed spatial data index in a multidimensional space to find nearby objects with which direct connections can be formed \cite{douglas}. This extension to a multidimensional peer-to-peer network can be useful for a real 3D overlay, associated with other overlays based on social, semantic, or any kind of proximity.
